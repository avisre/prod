# StockPortfolio.pro competitor market map

**Evidence snapshot:** 21 August 2026 (IST)

**Scope:** public product, pricing, API and positioning pages. Prices are USD unless a source says otherwise; promotional prices, taxes and billing terms can change.

## Executive answer

**Observed fact:** StockPortfolio.pro is entering a crowded market. Cheap or free products already cover broad financial data, screeners and portfolios; specialist products already offer filing search; several newer products already offer cited AI, APIs and MCP access.

**Strategic inference:** “AI stock research with sources” is not a defensible category by itself. The narrowest credible wedge is:

> **Know what changed. Check the evidence in the filing.**

That positions StockPortfolio.pro as a low-cost verification and change-detection workflow for serious self-directed US-equity investors. It should not try to out-breadth global terminals, out-chart TradingView, or promise an API/MCP platform before demand and licensing are proven.

#### What the added evidence changes (inference)

1. **The closest direct substitute is even clearer.** FinChat is not a separate active competitor; it became Fiscal.ai, which already combines AI, notifications, filings, screening, portfolio analysis, export and developer access. StockPortfolio.pro cannot win by presenting that bundle as novel.
2. **Retention is attached to a repeated trigger.** Seeking Alpha, Simply Wall St, GuruFocus, MarketScreener and New Constructs all use some combination of portfolios, alerts, newsletters, ratings changes or recurring research. This strengthens—but does not prove—the case for testing a reliable “what changed?” loop.
3. **High prices correspond to differentiated data or workflow depth.** New Constructs charges far more when proprietary forensic adjustments, analyst validation, marked-up filings and professional workflows are included. A higher StockPortfolio.pro tier needs a similarly concrete outcome, not merely more Ask calls.
4. **Public page volume is not a moat by itself.** Seeking Alpha, Simply Wall St, GuruFocus and MarketScreener already expose large public research surfaces. StockPortfolio.pro's organic pages need a distinctly useful answer and evidence trail; page count alone is unlikely to create durable advantage.
5. **The recommended position still holds.** None of the added evidence makes “broad terminal” or “AI stock research” a less crowded claim. The narrower verification/change-detection promise remains the more testable opening.

## Recommended market position

**Observed facts:**

- The SEC itself presents EDGAR as a free research source for company, fund and ETF filings. [SEC Investor.gov EDGAR guide](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/getting-started/researching-investments/using-edgar-research-investments)
- StockPortfolio.pro has only one public review and nine observed AppSumo orders in the internal audit; no direct subscription channel has yet demonstrated retained revenue.
- One buyer review emphasizes efficiency while still wanting to verify accuracy; buyer questions include ETF support, plan mapping, reliability, export/API/MCP and logical alerts.

**Inference:** the most defensible initial position is not “better financial data.” It is a productized shortcut from a research question to a verifiable filing trail:

1. Enter a ticker or holding.
2. See what changed or what looks non-obvious.
3. Read the answer with period and calculation attached.
4. Open the filing evidence.
5. Save or monitor the result.

The commercial message should make the verification event visible in under 60 seconds. The AI is the mechanism; confidence in an important research claim is the outcome.

## Product and go-to-market implications

1. **Protect the narrow ICP.** Prioritize serious self-directed investors researching US-listed companies. Do not lead with advisors, technical traders, global-market users or developers.
2. **Use a proof-first demo.** Show one non-obvious filed change and the click-through source. Avoid a feature montage.
3. **Make the competitor contrast factual.** “Source attached to the answer” is safe. “Never hallucinates,” “every number is filed,” or universal accuracy claims are not necessary and are difficult to defend across derived values and non-stock data.
4. **Sell the recurring job.** “Know what changed” gives a reason to return; a one-off answer does not. This remains a hypothesis until repeated use is observed.
5. **Do not build API/MCP from one request.** Run concierge/API discovery with 5–10 qualified prospects first; ask what action they automate, frequency, data rights, budget and acceptable latency.
6. **Do not chase feature parity.** Each broad competitor has years of data, licensing and workflow depth. A tight evidence path is cheaper to prove and easier to explain.

## What would change this conclusion

- Three or more paying customers repeatedly use monitoring/alerts and explicitly value it over Ask: lead with “what changed.”
- Three or more qualified teams commit budget for API/MCP with compatible data rights: consider an integration product.
- Search or behavioral evidence shows comparison/screener workflows drive paid conversion: lead with that observed path instead.
- Direct annual/Desk sales appear from professional users: revisit the ICP and enterprise workflow.

## WHY THIS MIGHT BE WRONG

1. The purchase sample is nine orders and the public review sample is one. A single buyer can materially change percentages.
2. AppSumo buyers may optimize for lifetime-deal value rather than represent the durable subscription ICP.
3. Most acquisition sources and pre-purchase behavior are not observable, so the proposed wedge is inferred from questions and post-purchase use—not a proven purchase cause.
4. Competitor pages are vendor-authored and describe capabilities, not independently measured quality, retention or customer satisfaction.
5. Prices and entitlements can change, may be localized, and are not directly comparable across annual, monthly, lifetime and enterprise contracts.
6. “What changed” may be a compelling message but operationally demanding; stale or noisy alerts would damage trust faster than a missing feature.
7. Source citations can increase perceived trust even when the citations are poor. The product must make the *quality and relevance* of the evidence inspectable, not merely display links.
